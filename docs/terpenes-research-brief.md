# Terpenes in *Artemisia* species

**Research brief — 1 September 2026**

## Executive summary

*Artemisia* is a large and chemically heterogeneous genus. Its characteristic odors generally come from volatile terpenes and terpenoids concentrated in leaves, flower heads, and other aerial tissues, but there is no single “*Artemisia* terpene profile.” The profile depends on species, population or chemotype, tissue, developmental stage, environment, harvest timing, drying, and extraction method.

The most important analytical distinction is between:

1. **Volatile essential-oil constituents:** chiefly monoterpenes (C10) and sesquiterpenes (C15), including camphor, 1,8-cineole, thujones, borneol, terpinen-4-ol, germacrene D, β-caryophyllene, davanone, and chamazulene.
2. **Nonvolatile specialized terpenoids:** especially sesquiterpene lactones such as artemisinin, which should not be treated as an essential-oil constituent merely because it is terpene-derived.
3. **Triterpenoids and other isoprenoids:** less volatile compounds associated with plant surfaces and defense, including compounds made in or associated with glandular trichomes.

The best-developed pathway is artemisinin biosynthesis in *A. annua*: FPP → amorpha-4,11-diene via amorpha-4,11-diene synthase (ADS), followed by CYP71AV1-mediated oxidation and downstream DBR2/ALDH1 steps toward dihydroartemisinic acid and artemisinin. The terminal conversion to artemisinin includes important nonenzymatic chemistry and is not fully resolved. Evidence from *A. annua* should not automatically be generalized to all *Artemisia* species.

## Chemical classes and representative compounds

| Class | Carbon skeleton | Representative *Artemisia* constituents | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Monoterpene hydrocarbons | C10 | α-pinene, β-pinene, camphene, limonene, myrcene, sabinene | Often abundant in fresh volatile fractions; readily affected by drying and distillation |
| Oxygenated monoterpenes | C10 | camphor, 1,8-cineole, borneol, thujone, terpinen-4-ol, linalool | Major odor and bioactivity contributors in many oils |
| Sesquiterpene hydrocarbons | C15 | germacrene D, β-caryophyllene, α-humulene, δ-cadinene, γ-cadinene | Commonly variable across populations and growth stages |
| Oxygenated sesquiterpenes | C15 | davanone, spathulenol, nerolidol, caryophyllene oxide | Prominent in some *A. absinthium*, *A. sieberi*, and related oils |
| Sesquiterpene lactones | C15 | artemisinin, artemisinic acid, lactucin-type and guaianolide compounds | Usually nonvolatile; extractable chemistry rather than ordinary essential-oil chemistry |
| Triterpenoids | C30 | cuticular and other specialized triterpenoids | Less volatile; relevant to aerial-surface protection and plant defense |

“Terpene” is used here for the hydrocarbon skeleton family; oxygenated or otherwise modified members are more precisely called terpenoids.

## Species-level evidence

### *Artemisia annua* (annual wormwood)

This is the molecular reference species because it produces artemisinin. Its essential oil can nevertheless be dominated by very different volatile profiles in different studies: reported major constituents include artemisia ketone, camphor, germacrene D, and 1,8-cineole. Those values are not a universal species signature; they are observations from particular genetic material, tissues, environments, and extraction protocols.

The artemisinin pathway begins with farnesyl diphosphate (FPP), a C15 precursor. ADS cyclizes FPP to amorpha-4,11-diene. CYP71AV1 oxidizes amorpha-4,11-diene and related intermediates; DBR2 and ALDH1 help route the pathway through dihydroartemisinic acid. Glandular secretory trichomes are central biosynthetic and storage sites. Jasmonate-responsive transcriptional networks regulate several pathway genes, linking terpene production to environmental and developmental signaling. A mutant study also showed that blocking CYP71AV1 redirects metabolism toward the sesquiterpene epoxide arteannuin X and that nonenzymatic conversion contributes materially to the pathway.

### *Artemisia absinthium* (wormwood)

Wormwood demonstrates why “the terpene content of a species” is an unsafe simplification. Depending on provenance and sampling, oils may be thujone-, camphor-, 1,8-cineole-, germacrene D-, davanone-, or other chemotypes. In one Saudi Arabian aerial-part study, the oil contained 44.9% oxygenated sesquiterpenes and 26.7% oxygenated monoterpenes; cis-davanone was 34.7%, camphor 16.2%, and chamazulene 8.2% of the oil. A seasonal study from Iran found substantial changes in composition across harvest stages and identified chamazulene as an important contributor to measured antioxidant activity.

Thujone is a safety-relevant monoterpene ketone, but it is not present at a fixed level in every wormwood sample. The European Medicines Agency states that thujone is neurotoxic, recommends low-thujone chemotypes, and warns against assuming traditional use establishes safety or efficacy. Any project record should therefore store thujone as an observed, quantified analyte tied to a specimen or preparation—not as a genus-wide property.

### *Artemisia sieberi* and *A. scoparia*

A comparative Saudi Arabian study illustrates divergence even among sympatric or regionally associated species. *A. sieberi* oil was rich in oxygenated sesquiterpenes (41.3%) and oxygenated monoterpenes (39.5%), with cis-davanone at 36.1% and camphor at 24.1%. In contrast, *A. scoparia* oil was dominated by non-terpenoid ketones: 2-nonanone (55%) and 2-undecanone (24.5%). This is an important negative finding: an *Artemisia* plant can be strongly aromatic without its oil being terpene-dominated.

### *Artemisia herba-alba*

Published oils commonly emphasize oxygenated monoterpenes, with regional reports describing combinations of 1,8-cineole, thujones, camphor, borneol, and terpinen-4-ol. The exact major compounds vary by region and chemotype. Reports of antimicrobial or insecticidal effects should be kept linked to the tested oil composition; they cannot be transferred reliably to an unidentified *A. herba-alba* product.

### *Artemisia dracunculus* (tarragon)

Tarragon is a useful counterexample to the assumption that all *Artemisia* oils are camphor/thujone types. Some populations produce oils dominated by estragole (p-allylanisole), with ocimenes and limonene also reported. Estragole is not a terpene, so it belongs in a broader volatile-metabolite record rather than a terpene-only table. The species entry should preserve this distinction.

### *Artemisia vulgaris* and *A. argyi*

Mugwort and Chinese mugwort show strong geographic and tissue-level variation. Published profiles include 1,8-cineole, camphor, borneol, chrysanthenyl acetate, α-humulene, δ-cadinene, caryophyllene oxide, and thujone among the reported major compounds. These species are good candidates for a future standardized comparison because leaves, flower buds, essential oils, and moxa preparations are not chemically interchangeable.

## Biosynthesis and compartmentalization

All terpenoids derive from the universal C5 building blocks IPP and DMAPP. In plants, the plastidial MEP pathway generally supplies monoterpene precursors, while the cytosolic mevalonate pathway supplies much of the sesquiterpene precursor FPP; cross-talk and species-specific exceptions exist. Terpene synthases convert prenyl diphosphates into hydrocarbon scaffolds, after which cytochrome P450s, dehydrogenases, reductases, oxidases, acyltransferases, and nonenzymatic reactions diversify them.

In *A. annua*, glandular secretory trichomes compartmentalize much of this chemistry. A trichome cDNA study isolated and functionally characterized monoterpene synthases, and wounding or phytohormone treatments changed their expression. This supports a role in plant–environment interactions, but gene expression or in-vitro enzyme activity is not by itself proof of field-level ecological function.

## Why reported percentages disagree

Differences among papers can be biological or analytical:

- population genetics and stable chemotypes;
- plant part and trichome density;
- plant age, flowering stage, and season;
- altitude, water status, soil, and temperature;
- fresh versus dried material;
- hydrodistillation versus solvent, headspace, or supercritical-fluid extraction;
- GC–MS library assignments, retention-index confirmation, and quantification conventions.

For reproducible records, capture voucher or accession information, collection coordinates, date and phenological stage, plant part, extraction method, oil yield, analytical platform, identification confidence, and whether percentages are normalized peak areas or absolute concentrations.

## Evidence and safety boundaries

Essential-oil antimicrobial, antioxidant, insecticidal, or cytotoxic assays are generally in-vitro findings. They do not establish clinical efficacy, safe human dose, or that one constituent is responsible for the activity. Whole oils can show additive, synergistic, antagonistic, or matrix effects.

The strongest medicinal evidence in this genus concerns artemisinin-derived antimalarial treatment, not generalized use of *Artemisia* essential oils. Artemisinin is also not equivalent to crude *A. annua* tea or essential oil. For wormwood and other thujone-containing materials, safety assessment must be preparation-specific and dose-specific; pregnant people and people with relevant liver, biliary, neurological, or allergy concerns require professional guidance.

## Recommended next research steps

1. Build a specimen-level dataset rather than a single species-average table.
2. Separate volatile oils, solvent extracts, and purified compounds in the schema.
3. Add a chemotype vocabulary: dominant compound or class, locality, tissue, harvest stage, and analytical method.
4. Use Kew Plants of the World Online for accepted names and synonyms before joining chemistry records.
5. Add primary sequence and pathway records for *A. annua* ADS, CYP71AV1, DBR2, ALDH1, and relevant terpene synthases.
6. Treat ecological and pharmacological claims as hypotheses unless supported by appropriate organism-level or clinical evidence.

### Evidence grading used here

- **High:** enzyme characterization, genome/pathway studies, or official regulatory monographs.
- **Moderate:** replicated analytical studies with clear specimen and method metadata.
- **Contextual:** narrative reviews or biological-activity assays that help generate hypotheses but do not establish generality or clinical efficacy.
